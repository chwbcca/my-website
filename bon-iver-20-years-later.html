Bon Iver, 20 Years Later

June 2026

I am on a bed. It is a terrible bed, let’s be honest[cite: 1]. I am in what you would call a big room for someone of modest means[cite: 2]. Underfoot, the carpet is a faded green, patterned with triangles and stained by a history that dates back twenty, maybe thirty years[cite: 3]. The lightbulbs are mostly missing; the one in the bathroom is conveniently broken, leaving a lone, surviving bulb to cast its light from afar[cite: 4].

My things are strewn across the floor[cite: 5]. I have stuffed a couch blanket into the gap beneath the front door, which lets in entirely too much ambient noise, light, and, above all, cigarette smoke from the hallway[cite: 5]. I am stationed right next to the reception area, and the smoke pours in relentlessly as the owner drinks, smokes, and makes noise with his friends[cite: 6]. A couple of velour-covered armchairs sit in the corner, looking as though they arrived straight from the Soviet Union with love[cite: 7]. There are two radiators: a new one that doesn’t work, and an old one that doesn’t work either[cite: 8]. The rest of the furniture is constructed from composite wood—some of it stained, some of it fragile, and all of it thoroughly depressing[cite: 9].

Staring at the walls, tracing the architecture of old spaces.

As I sit here reading through my old stories, I can’t stop thinking about grim days in Nanjing, marked by endless, heavy downpours of rain[cite: 10]. Or perhaps it was Perth[cite: 11]? The circumstances were much the same: missing my friends, reeling from consecutive breakups with two wonderful women, and having stubbornly told everyone I went to school with that I wanted absolutely nothing to do with them[cite: 11, 12]. I remember sleeping on a couch in someone else's living room, watching non-stop reruns of my favorite show just to try and force a bit of cheer into myself[cite: 13].

She would have known I was going deep[cite: 14]. She would have known I was already deep to begin with[cite: 14].

And now, here I am[cite: 14]. I sit in a room that smells of tobacco smoke and old, degrading plastic[cite: 15]. Looking at the clash of purple, blue, green, and yellow paint on the walls, I feel a profound wave of sadness[cite: 16]. Sadness for what was[cite: 16]. Sadness for what never came to be[cite: 17]. Sadness for what is today[cite: 17].

Today is simply me: resting in a cold room with no heat, smelling of stale smoke, isolated in a strange city full of people who don’t smile, quietly wondering what comes next[cite: 18, 19].
